# best dog food: how to choose by age, size and stomach, and pay less on every autoship reorder

A 75-pound Labrador eating a 34-pound bag every five weeks will go through roughly 21 bags in two years. At about $92 a bag, that's $1,932 of dog food before you've bought a leash, a dental chew, or a single vet visit.

That number is the reason "best dog food" is a harder question than most lists make it look. Nearly every ranking you'll find stops at the brand name. Very few do the arithmetic on what that brand costs per month, per year, per dog, or explain that the same bag carries a different effective price depending on whether you buy it once, subscribe to it, or route the purchase through a cashback layer.

So treat this as two guides stacked on each other. First, what the veterinary literature actually says about picking a formula. Second, how the mechanics of ordering it change what you pay.

## The signals that separate a solid formula from a well-marketed one

Veterinary sources converge on roughly the same checklist, and almost none of it is on the front of the bag.

**An AAFCO statement naming a life stage.** AAFCO doesn't approve or certify foods. The statement on the package simply confirms the food meets nutrient requirements for a designated life stage — growth, adult maintenance, or all life stages.

**How that claim was substantiated.** Some recipes are verified through actual feeding trials with real dogs; others are calculated on paper from ingredient data. Feeding trials are the stronger evidence.

**A board-certified veterinary nutritionist in the room.** Look for a DACVN (Diplomate of the American College of Veterinary Nutrition) or an equivalent PhD animal nutritionist employed or directly contracted by the brand for recipe formulation and review, not just listed on an advisory board.

**Public, specific answers to WSAVA's manufacturer-selection questions.** WSAVA publishes the questions it recommends vets ask brands about ingredient sourcing, manufacturing facilities, batch testing, and contamination protocols. It does not certify brands or keep a compliant-brand list. What matters is whether a manufacturer answers those questions substantively in public.

**Recall history and ingredient transparency.** Named animal proteins rather than vague "meat by-products," traceable sourcing, and a published guaranteed analysis.

What doesn't count: *holistic*, *premium*, *human-grade*, *ancestral*. As one veterinarian put it in Healthline's vet-reviewed guide:

> "Many dog food labels use language like 'holistic,' 'premium,' 'human-grade,' or 'ancestral,' but these words don't guarantee good nutrition. Look for meaningful signs instead, like AAFCO statements, life-stage designations, and feeding trial validations, which show the food is truly balanced for your dog."

## Matching the food to the dog in front of you

**Puppies.** Feed a diet labelled for growth until your dog reaches adult body weight. Small breeds generally switch to adult food at seven to nine months, larger breeds at 12 to 14 months, and giant breeds can stay on a puppy diet up to around 18 months because they keep growing longer.

**Large and giant breeds.** Look for formulas that carry glucosamine and chondroitin alongside controlled calcium and phosphorus levels. Joint problems are common enough in big dogs that it's a design consideration, not a marketing line.

**Sensitive stomachs.** Two different products get confused here. Over-the-counter sensitive-skin-and-stomach formulas are a reasonable first attempt for occasional soft stools. Diagnosed conditions — allergies, inflammatory bowel disease, chronic GI disease — belong on a prescription veterinary diet under veterinary direction, not on a shelf pick.

**Seniors.** If your older dog is healthy and eating a balanced diet, Tufts' guidance doesn't call for changing the food just because of age. Change it if the dog's condition, weight, or kidney function changes.

**The grain-free question.** This one deserves a straight answer. The FDA opened an investigation in 2019 into a possible link between certain grain-free diets and canine dilated cardiomyopathy, and identified 16 pet food brands in that scope, including Merrick and Acana. No foods have been recalled as a result, and the investigation has continued without a final resolution. If your dog is doing well on a grain-free diet for a documented reason, that's a conversation with your vet. If you picked grain-free because it sounds healthier, there's no evidence behind the upgrade.

**Body condition, not the bag.** The feeding chart on the back is calculated for a normal, active dog. Plenty of dogs aren't. Use a body condition score — a 4 or 5 out of 9 is the target — and adjust portions to that.

**Dry or wet?** Kibble is around 10% water; canned food is upward of 70%. If both products have comparable ingredients, the difference is largely hydration and palatability. Wet food helps dogs who can't chew kibble comfortably, dogs with urinary issues, and picky eaters. It costs more per calorie and doesn't keep once opened.

**Switching foods.** Rushing this is the most common self-inflicted problem. Feed 75% old food and 25% new for the first few days, move to 50/50 for the next two or three, then 75% new for a few days, and go to 100% after about a week.

## Common questions

**Is an expensive dog food actually better?**
Not automatically. What the veterinary framework rewards is substantiation, nutritionist involvement, and manufacturing controls — not price. A $25.97 bag from a large manufacturer with feeding trials behind it can be a more defensible choice than a $76 boutique bag with no published trials. Price tracks ingredient sourcing and protein content, which are real things, but they're not the same as proof of nutritional adequacy.

**How much should I actually feed?**
Start with the label, then adjust to body condition. The label assumes a normal, active dog. Obesity is the most common nutritional disease in dogs, and per figures cited in NBC Select's reporting, in some breeds it can take as much as two years off a lifespan. Watch the dog, not the scoop.

Yes on eligible Chewy orders, including repeat Autoship deliveries, at whatever rate is published on the merchant page at the time. It does not apply to pharmacy or prescription items, and it takes around 90 days to confirm on Chewy specifically.
